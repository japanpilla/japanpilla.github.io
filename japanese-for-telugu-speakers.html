Why Telugu Speakers Can Learn Japanese Fast

By japanpilla, a Telugu creator based in Tokyo · Updated October 2026

Most Japanese lessons are written for English speakers, and they treat Japanese as a completely foreign language. For a Telugu speaker the picture is very different. Telugu and Japanese are not related, but they share several features of grammar and sound. This lesson shows where Telugu helps you, where it gets in the way, and how to start reading hiragana.

1. Same word order: subject, object, verb

English puts the verb in the middle: "I eat rice". Telugu and Japanese both put the verb last: నేను అన్నం తింటాను. Japanese does the same thing:

JapaneseRomajiEnglishతెలుగుWhat to notice
私はご飯を食べます。Watashi wa gohan o tabemasu.I eat rice.నేను అన్నం తింటాను.Subject → object → verb. The same order as Telugu.
私は明日駅に行きます。Watashi wa ashita eki ni ikimasu.I will go to the station tomorrow.నేను రేపు స్టేషన్‌కి వెళ్తాను.Time first, then place, then the verb at the end.
私はハイデラバードから来ました。Watashi wa Haidarabaado kara kimashita.I came from Hyderabad.నేను హైదరాబాద్ నుండి వచ్చాను.から (kara) matches నుండి.
テルグ語を話しますか？Terugugo o hanashimasu ka?Do you speak Telugu?మీరు తెలుగు మాట్లాడతారా?Both languages end a question with a question sound (ka / -ా).

English speakers must reverse their thinking. You do not. Once you know the words, building sentences feels natural.

2. Particles work like Telugu endings

Telugu attaches short endings to words, such as -ను, -కి, -తో, -నుండి, to show their role in a sentence. Japanese does the same with small words called particles. They come after the word they mark, just like Telugu endings.

にniTo / at (time, place)-కి / -కు駅に行きます。(Eki ni ikimasu.) I go to the station.

Be careful: the matches are close, not exact. Japanese は and が both mark a subject in different ways, and the choice between them takes time to learn. Treat the Telugu comparison as a memory aid, not a rule.

3. Sounds that match, and sounds that do not

Where Telugu helps:

  • Five clean vowels. Japanese a, i, u, e, o are close to అ, ఇ, ఉ, ఎ, ఒ. English speakers struggle with this, and you do not.
  • Long vs short vowels. Telugu has both, and Japanese uses them to change meaning, such as obasan (aunt) and obaasan (grandmother).
  • Double consonants. Telugu already has them in words like అక్క and చెల్లి. Japanese kitte (stamp) is the same idea.
  • Dental sounds. The Japanese t and d are close to Telugu త and ద, not to the hard English t.

Where Telugu can trip you:

  • Japanese has no aspirated sounds like ఖ or ఘ, so keep ka and ga plain.
  • The sound tsu (つ) and fu (ふ) have no exact Telugu letter. Listen and repeat.
  • Japanese has a pitch accent: words rise and fall in pitch. 橋 (hashi, bridge) and 箸 (hashi, chopsticks) differ only in pitch. Listen to native speakers and copy them.

4. Japan's three scripts

  • Hiragana (46 basic characters): used for grammar and native words. Learn this first.
  • Katakana (46 characters): used for foreign words like インド (India), アニメ (anime) and コスプレ (cosplay).
  • Kanji (thousands of characters): borrowed from Chinese, used for the main meaning of words. Learn gradually, starting with common ones like 日, 人, 大, 水.

Unlike Telugu, which writes consonant and vowel together in one letter, Japanese kana each represent a whole syllable. This makes it simple: one character, one sound.

With these 25 characters you can already read words like あい (ai, love), いえ (ie, house), かさ (kasa, umbrella), すし (sushi) and なに (nani, what).

Practice tip: write each character 10 times while saying the sound. Stroke order matters, so look at a stroke-order chart or watch a short video when you start.

6. A simple study plan

  1. Week 1: hiragana rows 1 to 5 and the phrases in Everyday Japanese.
  2. Week 2: remaining hiragana, plus numbers and prices.
  3. Week 3: katakana, plus restaurant and train sentences.
  4. Week 4: make five simple sentences a day using は, を, に and で.
  5. Every day: listen to 10 minutes of Japanese, anime or podcasts.
