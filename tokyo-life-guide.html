Life in Tokyo for Telugu Speakers: Practical Guide to Culture, Food, Housing and Daily Rules

By japanpilla, a Telugu creator based in Tokyo · Updated October 2026

Moving from South India to Tokyo is exciting, and it is also full of small surprises. Tokyo is clean, safe and efficient, but daily life runs on unwritten rules that nobody explains. This guide covers what newcomers from Telugu-speaking families most often ask about: getting around, food, housing, paperwork, weather and safety.

Please note: this is general information based on daily life in Tokyo, not legal, visa or financial advice. Rules, fees and schedules change and differ between city wards. For anything official, check the website of the Immigration Services Agency of Japan, your employer or school, or your local ward office.

1. Commuting and the quiet-train rule

Trains in Tokyo are quiet. People do not take phone calls on trains, they speak in low voices and they put their phones on silent (manner mode). Newcomers are often surprised that a packed carriage can be almost silent. Follow the same habits and nobody will look twice at you.

  • Get a Suica or PASMO IC card on your first day. It works on trains, buses and in many shops. See our station Japanese lesson.
  • Most lines stop around midnight, so a late evening needs planning. Taxis are available but expensive.
  • Avoid the morning rush if you can, between about 7:30 and 9:30, when major lines are very crowded.
  • Many people walk or cycle for short distances. Cycle parking is regulated, so park only in marked areas.

2. Food, groceries and vegetarian eating

Finding familiar ingredients is the question I hear most from Telugu speakers. The good news is that it is possible. Areas such as Nishi-Kasai are well known for their Indian community and grocery shops, and neighbourhoods like Shin-Okubo have many international and halal food stores. You can usually find rice, dal, spices and ready masala. Stock changes from shop to shop, so call or check before making a long trip. Some Indian grocers also deliver online.

  • Regular supermarkets (Life, Maruetsu, OK and others) are the cheapest for vegetables, eggs and rice.
  • Curry leaves, tamarind and specialty pickles are easiest from Indian shops, not regular supermarkets.
  • Discounts after 7 or 8 pm. Many supermarkets put stickers on bentos and fresh food that day, which is a good way to save money.
  • Small kitchens. Many apartments have one burner or an induction cooker. Pressure cookers and a rice cooker are very useful.

Eating out as a vegetarian is possible but needs care, because a lot of Japanese cooking uses fish stock (dashi). Our restaurant Japanese lesson explains how to ask about it. Learn the characters 魚 (fish), 肉 (meat) and 卵 (egg) so that you can read ingredient lists.

3. Trash and recycling

Garbage sorting is serious in Japan. Typical categories are burnable waste, non-burnable waste, plastic containers, PET bottles, cans and glass, and paper and cardboard. But the details are set by each municipal ward, so the same item can go into different bins in Shinjuku and in Setagaya. Each ward publishes a calendar that tells you which bag goes out on which morning.

  • Use the official garbage bags if your ward requires them. They are sold in supermarkets and konbini.
  • Put trash out only on the correct morning, usually before 8:30, and at your building's collection point.
  • Rinse bottles and containers. Remove caps and labels where required.
  • Ask your landlord or neighbours if you are unsure. Mistakes are often left with a note attached.

4. Finding a home and what it really costs

Rent is usually the largest monthly cost in Tokyo. The system is different from India, and the first payment can be several months of rent. Typical initial costs include:

  • Deposit (shikikin), part of which may be returned when you move out.
  • Key money (reikin), a non-refundable gift to the landlord in some contracts.
  • Agency fee paid to the real estate agent.
  • Guarantor company fee, because many landlords require a guarantor.
  • Fire insurance and sometimes a key exchange fee.

Some real estate agents specialise in helping foreign residents, and some employers or universities arrange housing for newcomers. Apartments are small, so ask for the size in square metres and the floor plan, written as 1K or 1LDK. Remember that you take your shoes off at the entrance, and many buildings have rules about noise and visitors.

5. Paperwork after you arrive

  • Residence card. You usually receive it at major airports when you arrive for a long stay. Carry it with you.
  • Address registration. New residents register their address at the local ward office within 14 days of moving in. Bring your passport and residence card.
  • Health insurance. Residents are covered under Japan's public health insurance system, through an employer or the national scheme. Your ward office or employer will explain which one applies.
  • Bank account and phone. These need your residence card and address, and some providers take a few weeks. Ask your employer or school which banks they recommend.

6. Cash, cards and tipping

Japan is becoming more cashless, but cash is still useful at small restaurants, shrines and local shops. Convenience store ATMs accept many foreign cards. Do not tip. It is not expected, and staff may try to return the money.

7. Weather and seasons

Tokyo has four clear seasons. Spring brings cherry blossoms, usually from late March to early April. Summer is hot and very humid, so light cotton clothes and a small towel or fan help. Autumn has pleasant weather and colourful leaves, and typhoons can arrive in late summer and autumn. Winter is cold and dry with a lot of sunshine, but it rarely snows heavily in central Tokyo. If you come from Hyderabad, plan for a real winter coat.

8. Earthquakes and safety

Earthquakes happen regularly in Japan. Most are small, and buildings are designed for them. Tokyo is also considered very safe for daily life, including at night, but ordinary common sense still applies.

  • Make sure your phone can receive emergency alerts.
  • Learn where your nearest evacuation area is, and keep some water, a flashlight and a power bank at home.
  • In an emergency, call 110 for police and 119 for fire or ambulance.
